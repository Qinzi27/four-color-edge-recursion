# Mathematical foundations

[中文完整版 / Chinese version](FOUNDATIONS.md)

This research model formalizes Qinzi27's idea of recording the colors on both sides of a dividing edge and recording successive splits with nested parentheses. The results below are elementary facts, established mathematical correspondences, or direct dynamic-programming consequences with self-contained proofs. This repository does not claim a new proof of the Four-Color Theorem or established novelty for these results.

## 1. Maps, face identities, and history

The primary object is a finite connected graph embedded on the oriented sphere, with a distinguished outer face when viewed in the plane. Parallel edges and loops are allowed. Edges may be curved but their interiors do not intersect. Distinct regions are adjacent only when they share an edge, not merely a point. Disconnected maps, infinite maps, and disconnected regions assigned one compulsory common color are outside this version.

A nontrivial combinatorial map is $M=(D,\alpha,\sigma,f_\infty)$. Here $D$ is the dart set; $\alpha$ is a fixed-point-free involution pairing opposite darts; and $\sigma$ orders the outgoing darts at each vertex counterclockwise. Permutations act from right to left. Vertices, edges, and faces are respectively the orbits of $\sigma$, $\alpha$, and

$$
\phi=\sigma^{-1}\circ\alpha.
$$

The orbit containing dart $d$ is its left face $L(d)$; its right face is $R(d)=L(\alpha(d))$. Reversal exchanges the two faces. Require the action generated by $\alpha,\sigma$ to be transitive and the Euler characteristic $|V|-|E|+|F|$ to equal 2. Rotation systems alone may describe higher-genus orientable surfaces. The singleton map with no darts is treated separately as one vertex and one face.

Let $K=\mathbb Z_2^2=\{00,01,10,11\}$, with XOR as addition. The implementation represents these values by integers 0 through 3. A face coloring is $c:F\to K$; its edge-side signature is

$$
\Sigma(d)=\langle(L(d),c(L(d)))\mid(R(d),c(R(d)))\rangle.
$$

A coloring is proper when $L(d)\ne R(d)$ implies $c(L(d))\ne c(R(d))$. Face identities are essential: a dangling edge has the same face on both sides and must not impose a self-inequality constraint.

A model state is $(M,c,H)$, with $H$ an operation history. The expression $(1,(2,3))$ records a split of a region formerly colored 2. Actual resulting boundary segments may have signatures $(1,2),(1,3),(2,3)$. A parenthesis tree alone omits boundary occurrence order, vertex identifications, rotations, and later gluing. Historical nodes should use face identities, with color changes recorded explicitly.

## 2. Elementary embedded operations

An Extend operation inserts a fresh leaf vertex and an edge from a specified corner of an existing vertex into that corner's face. The new arc meets no old edge in its interior. It has $(\Delta V,\Delta E,\Delta F)=(1,1,0)$. Its tip can be passed within the face, so both shores remain the same face.

A CloseSplit operation adds an embedded arc between two specified boundary occurrences of the same face. Its interior is disjoint from the old map and it must separate that face into exactly two faces. In a disk with a simple closed boundary, a proper arc between distinct boundary points has this property. When a boundary revisits a vertex, corners must be specified; endpoint names alone are insufficient. Insert the new darts at the corresponding rotation positions. With endpoints already vertices, $(\Delta V,\Delta E,\Delta F)=(0,1,1)$. The separation condition proves the face increment; other faces are untouched. If an endpoint is inside an old edge, subdivide first, with increment $(1,1,0)$.

These operations preserve the Euler characteristic. Their topological validity does not imply that every split admits a new color while all old colors remain fixed.

A triangle boundary is a valid initial two-face example. A lone finite segment has only one face, so it is not an initial two-color partition. An infinite-line sketch must be interpreted through a surrounding boundary or spherical compactification appropriate to the example.

## 3. Edge differences and closed walks in the dual

Define $x_e=c(L(e))\oplus c(R(e))$. Differences are orientation-independent although ordered side signatures are not. A proper coloring gives nonzero differences on edges separating distinct faces and zero differences on bridges.

Let $G=M^*$ be the full dual multigraph, with original faces as vertices. A primal bridge yields a dual loop. Such a loop imposes no proper-coloring inequality but must have difference zero.

For a closed walk $W$ in the **dual graph**, define

$$
\operatorname{Hol}_x(W)=\bigoplus_{e\text{ traversed by }W}x_e,
$$

counting multiplicities. This is not a condition on arbitrary primal closed walks. For example, the three edges of a primal triangle may all have difference 01 between its inner and outer faces, even though their primal-cycle XOR is 01.

**Theorem 3.1 (path independence).** On any finite connected multigraph $G$, edge labels $x:E(G)\to K$ have the form $x_{uv}=c(u)\oplus c(v)$ if and only if every closed walk has XOR zero. If realizable, there are exactly four such color assignments, differing by a common $K$ translation; prescribing one root color gives uniqueness.

**Proof.** Differences derived from colors telescope around a closed walk: every encountered vertex color occurs twice. Conversely, fix root $r$ with color $a$ and define $c(v)=a\oplus\bigoplus_{e\in P}x_e$ along any root-to-$v$ path $P$. Two such paths form a closed walk when one is reversed, so the definition is independent of the path. Extending a path by edge $uv$ gives the required difference equation. A loop is a closed walk of length one and must have label zero. If $c,c'$ realize the same differences, $c(u)\oplus c'(u)=c(v)\oplus c'(v)$ along every edge; connectedness makes this a global constant. There are four root choices. $\square$

**Corollary 3.2 (finite verification).** Reconstruct colors along a spanning tree and check every remaining edge. If all checks pass, Theorem 3.1 applies. If edge $uv$ fails, the tree path between $u$ and $v$ together with that edge is a closed-walk witness with nonzero XOR.

**Proof.** Tree edges satisfy the equations by construction, so passing all other edges yields a potential on the whole graph. For a failed edge, the tree path telescopes to $c(u)\oplus c(v)$, which differs from its label. $\square$

The verifier takes $O(|V|+|E|)$ graph and fixed-size group operations. A returned witness need not be a shortest cycle or a minimum unsatisfiable core.

## 4. Primal vertex defects and planar duality

For a primal vertex $v$, let $\partial x(v)$ be the XOR of labels of all darts with tail $v$. A loop contributes twice and cancels. A $K$-flow is a labeling with $\partial x(v)=0$ at every vertex.

**Theorem 4.1 (face potentials and flows).** On a finite connected spherical map, a labeling is a $K$-flow if and only if it is the difference of face colors. Neither direction requires nonzero labels or proper colors.

**Proof.** Given face colors, list the corner colors around a vertex as $a_1,\ldots,a_k,a_{k+1}=a_1$. Incident differences are $a_i\oplus a_{i+1}$, whose XOR is zero; repeated faces and loop incidences cancel by multiplicity. Conversely, XOR the zero defects over a vertex set $U$. Every internal edge is counted twice, leaving

$$
\bigoplus_{e\in\delta(U)}x_e=0.
$$

A simple dual cycle is a Jordan curve on the sphere. The crossed primal edges form the cut between primal vertices on its two sides, so that dual cycle has XOR zero. This includes dual loops and two-edge cycles. Splitting at repeated vertices decomposes any closed walk into simple cycles and backtracks; backtracks cancel. Thus all dual closed walks have zero XOR, and Theorem 3.1 reconstructs the face potential. $\square$

The sphere hypothesis matters: on a positive-genus surface, vertex conservation alone does not eliminate all noncontractible dual periods.

**Corollary 4.2.** Every flow is zero on a bridge, since the bridge is a one-edge cut. A nonbridge belongs to a primal cycle, whose two sides show that the edge has different incident faces. A bridge has the same face on both sides. Consequently, a proper face four-coloring of a bridgeless spherical map is equivalent to a nowhere-zero $K$-flow. For maps with bridges, the correct condition is zero on bridges and nonzero on other edges.

**Corollary 4.3 (cubic case).** At a cubic vertex, three nonzero labels XOR to zero if and only if they are 01,10,11 in some order. Indeed, two equal labels would force the third to be zero, and $01\oplus10\oplus11=0$. This is the classical Tait edge-coloring formulation.

These equivalences test or transform a given labeling. They do not supply a new construction of a nowhere-zero flow on every bridgeless planar graph.

## 5. Exact boundary extension and gluing

Here $G$ is a **coloring constraint graph**, normally the dual with loops removed: its vertices represent regions. Let $B\subseteq V(G)$ be an interface of identified vertices. Define

$$
\mathcal R(G,B)=\{\beta:B\to K:\beta\text{ extends to a proper coloring of }G\}.
$$

This is a relation of all extendable assignments, not one greedy choice. The empty interface has relation $\{()\}$ when the graph is colorable and the empty relation otherwise. The naive number of states is at most $4^{|B|}$.

**Theorem 5.1 (join and projection).** Suppose $G=G_1\cup G_2$, with no additional edges, and $I=V(G_1)\cap V(G_2)\subseteq B_1\cap B_2$. Shared vertices have identical IDs; every common edge has the same constraint. For $B\subseteq B_1\cup B_2$,

$$
\mathcal R(G,B)=
\pi_B\bigl(\mathcal R(G_1,B_1)\bowtie_I\mathcal R(G_2,B_2)\bigr).
$$

**Proof.** A coloring of the union restricts to two extendable interface assignments agreeing on $I$, proving one inclusion. Conversely, choose internal extensions of two matching interface states. The extensions agree on their entire intersection $I$, so their union is a function. Every edge belongs to a subgraph and is satisfied there. Restriction to $B$ proves the other inclusion. $\square$

Geometric gluing also requires compatible embedded boundary order and planarity; the relation theorem alone does not establish those conditions.

**Proposition 5.2 (a fixed boundary can fail).** Take a four-cycle $v_1v_2v_3v_4v_1$ and add one central vertex adjacent to all four rim vertices. The proper rim precoloring $(0,1,2,3)$ has no extension, although the graph is three-colorable.

**Proof.** The center sees all four colors and has none available. In contrast, rim colors $(0,1,0,1)$ and center color 2 form a proper three-coloring. There are $4\cdot3\cdot3+4\cdot3\cdot2\cdot2=84$ proper four-color rim assignments, counting separately whether opposite vertices $v_1,v_3$ have equal colors. Exactly $4!=24$ use all four colors and fail to extend; the remaining 60 extend. Of these, $4\cdot3=12$ alternate two colors and permit two center colors; the other 48 permit one. There are therefore $12\cdot2+48=72$ complete colorings. $\square$

This disproves the overstrong lemma that every proper four-precoloring of a planar boundary extends. It diagnoses a possible recursive assumption; it does not presume that the user's incompletely specified original rules assert that lemma.

Global color permutations in $S_4$ preserve coloring feasibility. Independently canonicalizing two child states does not establish their relative alignment. Retain a relative permutation or try the at most 24 permutations when joining. With fixed precolored vertices, only permutations preserving the fixed values are allowed. Equal-color patterns on a shared interface must agree. The four translations in Theorem 3.1 concern fixed edge differences and are not the full 24-element color-permutation group.

## 6. A recursive baseline: stacked triangulations

This section concerns **vertex coloring**. Start with a root triangle and repeatedly insert a new vertex inside a triangular face, adjacent to its three vertices. This gives a rooted stacked construction; after the first insertion its graph is $K_4$.

**Theorem 6.1.** Fix three different colors on the root triangle. Every finite prescribed stacked construction has a unique proper four-coloring. It is obtained by giving each new vertex the one color missing from its three neighbors.

**Proof.** The root is already colored. Inductively, the next new vertex sees the three mutually adjacent vertices of a triangle, hence three different colors. Exactly one of the four colors is available. Choosing it satisfies all newly inserted edges. Every full coloring restricts to the unique previous coloring and must use this same remaining color. Induction proves existence and uniqueness. $\square$

Given the valid insertion sequence, construction and a per-step certificate use $O(n)$ fixed-size operations and records. Without prescribed root colors there are $4\cdot3\cdot2=24$ colorings.

For a region-coloring interpretation, this vertex graph is the region adjacency graph of a dual map. Inserting a vertex into a face of the original boundary map instead divides that face into three; the two interpretations must not be interchanged. This baseline does not cover all planar graphs or arbitrary splitting orders.

## 7. Exact nearest-flow repair

For a finite multigraph $H$ and original labels $d:E(H)\to K$, define

$$
\rho(H,d)=\min_{\substack{x:E(H)\to K\setminus\{0\}\\\partial x=0}}
\sum_e\mathbf1[x_e\ne d_e],
$$

with minimum $+\infty$ if no target flow exists. This is Hamming distance to a nowhere-zero flow. It is not distance to an unconstrained potential that could assign zero differences everywhere. The mathematics permits initial zero labels; the current repair API accepts only initial labels 1,2,3.

**Cycle formula.** For a cycle $C$ with $m$ edges, let $N_C(a)$ count edges initially labeled $a$. Allow a one-edge loop or a two-edge parallel cycle. Then

$$
\rho(C,d)=m-\max_{a\ne0}N_C(a).
$$

**Proof.** Conservation at each degree-two vertex forces successive edge values to agree, so the whole cycle has one nonzero value $a$. A loop may independently take any nonzero value because it is counted twice. Choosing $a$ changes exactly $m-N_C(a)$ edges. $\square$

**Cactus formula.** A connected cactus multigraph has each edge in at most one cycle, allowing one- and two-edge cycle blocks. A bridge makes $\rho=+\infty$. Otherwise its edge-disjoint cycle blocks $C_i$ give

$$
\rho(H,d)=\sum_i\bigl(|E(C_i)|-\max_{a\ne0}N_{C_i}(a)\bigr).
$$

**Proof.** In a leaf block, all vertices except at most its attachment vertex have degree two in the remaining graph, forcing a constant cycle value. At the attachment the two cycle incidences cancel, so deleting that block preserves the remaining conservation constraints. Peel the block tree; a loop also contributes zero at its attachment. Conversely, independent nonzero constants on all cycles satisfy conservation. Edge-disjoint costs add. A singleton vertex has cost zero. $\square$

**Theta formula.** A theta graph consists exactly of three edge-disjoint, internally vertex-disjoint paths $P_1,P_2,P_3$ between distinct terminals $s,t$, each of positive length. With $N_i(a)$ counting label $a$ on $P_i$ and total edge count $m$,

$$
\rho(H,d)=m-\max_{(a_1,a_2,a_3)\in\operatorname{Perm}(01,10,11)}
\sum_iN_i(a_i).
$$

**Proof.** Degree-two internal vertices force a constant nonzero value on each path. Conservation at a terminal requires the three constants to XOR to zero, hence they must be the three distinct nonzero values. The six choices have the displayed numbers of unchanged edges. $\square$

## 8. A four-state two-terminal series-parallel recursion

A two-terminal series-parallel (TTSP) network is supplied with its decomposition tree. A leaf is one edge with distinct terminals. Series composition identifies only the first child's sink with the second child's source. Parallel composition identifies corresponding terminal pairs and nothing else. Child edge sets are disjoint, with unique edge IDs. Parallel edges are allowed; this grammar does not introduce loops. Recognition and decomposition of arbitrary input graphs are separate tasks.

For $q\in K$, let $F_N(q)$ be the minimum repair cost with nonzero edge values, conservation at every internal vertex, and XOR $q$ at the source. The sink XOR is also $q$: summing all vertex defects counts every edge twice, leaving the XOR of the two terminal defects. Zero is a permissible terminal state even though edge values must be nonzero.

For an edge with initial value $d_e$,

$$
F_e(q)=
\begin{cases}\mathbf1[q\ne d_e],&q\ne0,\\+\infty,&q=0.\end{cases}
$$

**Theorem 8.1.** The exact recurrences are

$$
F_{A S B}(q)=F_A(q)+F_B(q),\qquad
F_{A P B}(q)=\min_{a\oplus b=q}\bigl(F_A(a)+F_B(b)\bigr).
$$

The nearest everywhere-conserved flow on the whole network has value $\rho(N,d)=F_N(0)$.

**Proof.** The leaf constraint is its edge value. In series, conservation at the identified internal vertex forces the child terminal values to agree; that common value is the outer terminal value. Costs add on disjoint edges, and optimal child witnesses can be combined. In parallel, the outer terminal value is exactly the XOR of the two child terminal values. Every feasible parent restricts to such child solutions, and every matching pair combines to a feasible parent. Minimizing gives equality, and induction on the supplied decomposition proves all table entries. Requiring both outer terminals to conserve gives state zero. $\square$

This keeps the two terminals distinct. If instead they are identified as one vertex, their contributions cancel and the optimum for that different operation is $\min_qF_N(q)$; identification may create loops.

There are four entries per node and four candidate pairs per parallel entry. A binary decomposition with $m$ leaf edges has $2m-1$ nodes, hence $O(m)$ arithmetic operations. Costs are integers at most $m$ or infinity; a direct bit-complexity upper bound is $O(m\log(m+1))$. Store constant-size minimizing backpointers, then reconstruct one solution in $O(m)$ operations. Copying whole solution dictionaries at every node would invalidate this operation bound.

A returned assignment independently certifies feasibility and its Hamming cost. Optimality is justified by the recurrence proof or by checking the full DP certificate, not by the assignment alone. The method is a direct finite-state dynamic program and is not claimed to be a new algorithm.

## 9. Implementation and evidence scope

The reference implementation separates embedded maps, face coloring, difference reconstruction, boundary relations, and nearest-flow repair. Plane-map validation supports loops, parallel edges, bridges, and the singleton map; it rejects disconnected maps and rotation systems with non-spherical Euler characteristic.

The corner-based history module supports Extend and CloseSplit replay. Each step reports before/after counts, its parent face, and old-face-to-new-face descendant mappings obtained from the surviving darts. Face numbers are local to each reconstructed map; replay metadata relates successive steps. The corner API requires a nonempty initial map with existing darts, so it does not start from the singleton map. This is an explicit embedded construction history, not a persistent coloring solver or a proof that every split extends a fixed old coloring. Edge subdivision, an arbitrary user-drawn geometry importer, and a complete general recoloring engine are separate future work.

Mathematical claims are limited to their stated hypotheses. The finite tests compare implementations with independent exhaustive oracles on recorded small inputs; actual covered families, counts, and bounds belong in the validation report. Passing tests does not prove all planar cases or certify the software in a proof assistant.

Unproved research questions include smaller sufficient boundary states, stronger repair bounds, minimum conflict cores, and terminating/confluent local rewrite systems beyond the established baseline classes. No general constructive four-color theorem or originality claim is inferred from the notation or from these tests.
